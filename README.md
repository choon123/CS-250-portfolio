# CS-250: Software Development Lifecycle Reflection

## Introduction

This repository documents my learning in CS-250: Software Development Lifecycle at Southern New Hampshire University. Throughout this course, I explored software development methodologies, Agile principles, Scrum accountabilities, user stories, acceptance criteria, team communication, and project management practices.

The SNHU Travel project helped me examine how these concepts work together throughout the software development lifecycle. My work included analyzing user stories, considering implementation and testing, responding to changing priorities, and reflecting on how teams can improve their processes. This portfolio artifact and reflection demonstrate my understanding of the importance of user-focused requirements, iterative development, and effective collaboration.

## 1. How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I interpret user needs by identifying what users want to accomplish, understanding the problems they are trying to solve, and translating those needs into clear and testable software requirements. Before implementing a feature, I need to understand its purpose, expected behavior, and the conditions that would make it useful to the intended user. Clarifying these details early helps reduce assumptions, misunderstandings, and unnecessary rework.

User stories help connect technical development to user needs by describing a feature from the user's perspective. They communicate who needs a feature, what the user wants to accomplish, and why it provides value. Acceptance criteria make these expectations more specific and provide a basis for evaluating whether the implemented functionality meets the requirement.

In the SNHU Travel project, user stories addressed features such as displaying popular destinations, finding destinations within a price limit, and selecting a vacation type. These examples helped me understand how broad business goals can be divided into smaller requirements that can be prioritized, implemented, and tested. I also learned that writing a user story does not automatically mean the feature is complete. The team must evaluate the implementation against its acceptance criteria and confirm the required outcomes. User stories therefore support both a shared understanding of requirements and a more organized approach to delivering useful software.

## 2. How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I approach program development as an iterative process involving requirements analysis, planning, design, implementation, testing, evaluation, and improvement. Each stage contributes to the quality of the final solution, but development also requires revisiting earlier decisions when new information or changing requirements affect the project. I believe it is important to identify problems early, test functionality throughout development, and use feedback to guide improvements rather than assuming the first implementation will meet every need.

The SNHU Travel project helped me consider how an Agile team can respond when priorities change. For example, the project scenario shifted toward detox and wellness vacations while retaining the original deadline. This illustrated how priorities can be reassessed, useful existing functionality can be preserved, and affected requirements and test cases can be reviewed. It also demonstrated the importance of communicating scope trade-offs when the available time does not change.

In future development work, I hope to incorporate Agile practices such as sprint planning, prioritized product backlogs, clear acceptance criteria, regular team communication, sprint reviews, and retrospectives. I also want to emphasize continuous testing and a shared Definition of Done so that the team has clear expectations for what constitutes completed work. Sprint reviews can help stakeholders evaluate outcomes, while retrospectives can help the team identify practical improvements to its working process. I see these practices as ways to increase transparency, respond constructively to change, and deliver software that better meets user needs.

## 3. What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member in software development means communicating clearly, taking responsibility for assigned work, respecting different perspectives, and contributing to a shared objective. Software development requires collaboration because requirements, implementation decisions, testing, and project priorities are interconnected. A misunderstanding in one area can affect the work of others and create avoidable delays or rework.

A dependable team member follows through on commitments, shares relevant information, raises blockers early, asks questions when requirements are unclear, and remains open to constructive feedback. In an Agile environment, good teamwork also means being transparent about progress and challenges, participating in planning and review activities, and supporting continuous improvement rather than focusing exclusively on individual tasks.

The SNHU Travel coursework reinforced the importance of connecting requirements, implementation, and testing. For example, clarifying how personalized destinations should behave can help ensure that implementation decisions and test cases reflect the same expectations. This kind of communication helps prevent team members from making conflicting assumptions. I believe being a good team member means contributing to a respectful, accountable, and collaborative environment in which everyone can work toward delivering a reliable and useful product.

The Sprint Review and Retrospective document from my CS-250 final project examines the SNHU Travel scenario through the lens of Agile and Scrum practices. The document also distinguishes between what the available coursework demonstrates and what cannot be verified, such as whether every user story passed acceptance testing or whether a production deployment occurred. This distinction is important because sound software development decisions should be based on evidence rather than assumptions.

Together, this artifact and README reflect my understanding of how user needs, iterative development, testing, communication, and teamwork contribute to the software development lifecycle. They also provide a record of lessons I can apply to future academic projects and professional software development work.
